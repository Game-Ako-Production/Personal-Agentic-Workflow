# Game Revival vs. Preservation: IP Triage as Industry Direction

## The Observed Pattern

Franchises like Suikoden, Terranigma, Tales of Eternia, Resident Evil, Final Fantasy, and Silent Hill are returning via remakes and remasters rather than originals becoming directly available. This signals a strategic shift: the industry is solving game "availability" through **re-releases and reworks**, not by opening access to old titles.

## Why This Happens: Economic Incentives

Publishers favor re-release over direct access for clear reasons:

1. **Resale generates revenue; archival access does not.** Providing legacy access costs nothing operationally but prevents resale of that title. A remake or remaster is a new product with a new price point and new marketing cost-justification.

2. **It's more defensible than "we're blocking this to protect sales."** Publishers cite IP rights, business-sensitive information (server architecture), contractor-owned code, and security concerns — all real but also convenient justifications that apply universally, whether or not a remake is planned.

3. **Selective unblocking per-title is operationally harder than blanket no.** Reviving a 1990s game often requires untangling server code from live-service infrastructure, licensing resolutions (music, middleware), and finding clean legal rights to all components. Easier to say "no to all" than "yes to this one."

## The Definitional Shift: Preservation ≠ Triage

This is **not** game preservation in the archival sense. Preservation means keeping an artifact playable indefinitely as a historical record — the museum standard. What's actually happening is **IP triage**: publishers audit the back catalog, evaluate which franchises still have fanbase and brand equity, and reinvest in only the ones that clear the commercial viability bar.

### Preservation asks: "Is this historically important?"

### Triage asks: "Does this still have market potential?"

These answer differently. Suikoden gets revived because it has evangelists. A niche mid-90s tactical RPG with no sequel and no vocal community stays dormant.

## Why Triage is Defensible (vs. Archival Preservation)

The original consumption model for most 90s games was single-playthrough-then-shelved. Those games were never designed to last indefinitely; they were designed to be consumed once and moved on from. Extending the _brand's_ lifespan via remake is a legitimate second act, not a preservation failure. The counterfactual isn't "this game stays perpetually playable" — it's "this IP dies quietly and generates nothing."

Furthermore, a substantial remake (RE2, FF7R, Silent Hill 2) creates genuine new value through design labor, not manufactured scarcity. It's fair to call these "buying it again," but they're also different products with different systems, pacing, and sometimes narrative structure.

## Where Triage Fails: Two Hard Boundaries

**1. The Long Tail Below Commercial Viability**

Games without enough remaining fanbase or cultural weight never clear the triage bar. They stay inaccessible regardless of how healthy the remake pipeline looks for AAA franchises. Triage solves nothing for the mid-tier and niche back catalog — those titles simply vanish.

**2. Server-Dependent Live-Service Games**

Once a live-service title's servers are shut down, no remake strategy can bring that specific game back. The Crew is the canonical example: the client was never the whole game. Once Ubisoft's servers closed, the artifact is extinct, and no IP-revival cycle addresses it because the IP has no residual brand equity to reinvest in.

The only solution for live-service is preservation-by-design: ship with offline mode, client-authoritative fallback, or planned sunset + private-server release. That has to be a planning-phase decision, not a post-hoc retrofit.

## The Regulatory Landscape (as of September 2026)

The EU's Stop Destroying Videogames initiative gathered 1.29M verified signatures demanding publishers keep games in functional state after commercial end-of-life. The European Commission's June 2026 response: **no binding legislation**. Instead, voluntary industry code of conduct by end 2026.

**Justifications cited:**

- IP rights and copyright constraints
- Business-sensitive information (server architecture)
- Publisher costs
- Cybersecurity/safety risk

**Why this matters:** It's evidence that regulators see legal precedent in "force publishers to release access to copyrighted work" as higher-stakes than just "protect one company's remaster roadmap." Compulsory licensing for digital goods has implications far beyond gaming.

**Current status:** The fight continues. Stop Killing Games is pushing the issue into the EU Digital Fairness Act, California AB 1921 cleared a Senate committee hearing, and Dutch consumer groups filed suit against Sony over PlayStation Store pricing control. Not closed.

## The #2 Access Question (Separate from #1 Revenue)

There's a conflation in the discourse between:

- **#1: "Fund preservation" (unreasonable)** — asking publishers to budget for maintaining access to zero-revenue games. This competes with triage investment and asking for this is asking them to subsidize an archive.

- **#2: "Don't legally block access" (reasonable)** — asking publishers to stop using DMCA and IP law to prevent others (emulators, abandonware sites) from keeping commercially dead titles playable. This costs publishers nothing.

Publisher resistance to #2 is economically rational: blocking access protects optionality on future remasters and prevents cannibalization by free alternatives. But it's not evidence of a solved preservation problem — it's evidence that preservation isn't solved, just deferred to a fight over regulatory permission.

## Net Positive Assessment

**Yes, net positive — with clear limitations.**

**Positive because:**

- Genuine value creation for substantive reworks (not repackaging)
- Solves the "I own this on disc but no hardware to play it" problem without piracy
- Keeps IP alive commercially, which is the precondition for sequels, transmedia, cultural persistence
- The counterfactual to "Silent Hill 2 gets remade" is not "the original stays accessible forever" — it's "Silent Hill dies quiet"

**Limitations:**

- Only reaches the top 1-2% of the back catalog; the long tail gets nothing
- Doesn't solve live-service extinction (those games are gone regardless)
- Isn't a substitute for the access question — revival and preservation aren't competing for the same budget, but they're also not mutually exclusive policy requirements

**On the backlash:** Partly confused (conflating FF7R-scale reworks with minimal remasters), partly legitimate (absence of a parallel track for original access, publisher delisting of originals to clear room for remakes).

## Key Takeaway for Forward Development

For new games: **preservation must be a planning-phase decision, not post-hoc retrofit.** The issue isn't retrospectively saving the 90s catalog — it's building games now that can survive their commercial cycle without becoming extinct. That means offline modes, client-authoritative logic, documented sunset paths, and private-server tooling baked in from design, not added later as a negotiation point with regulators.

Triage solves IP monetization. Preservation-by-design solves the artifact-extinction problem. They're orthogonal, and the industry needs both.
