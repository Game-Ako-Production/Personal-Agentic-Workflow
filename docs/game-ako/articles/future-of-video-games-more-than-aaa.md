# The Future of Video Games Should Make Room for More Than AAA

## What do we mean when we call a game ambitious?

I sometimes wonder whether the video game industry has become too used to measuring ambition by scale: bigger worlds, more realistic graphics, more cinematic presentation. Those things can be impressive. But I worry that when commercial ambition is judged mainly by how much a game resembles a blockbuster, the medium’s possibilities can get crowded out—including the possibilities unique to playing a game.

That is my concern, not a claim that game design has been abandoned. A lavish production can also have distinctive mechanics, and a small game can be conventional. Still, I want us to ask whether technical spectacle and scale have become the easiest signs of ambition to recognize—and whether that makes it harder to value unusual ideas that do not need either.

My own route toward games shapes how I think about this. I once wanted to make them, but chose computer science and IT because a dedicated path did not feel readily available to me then. I can see now that a BS in Entertainment and Multimedia Computing exists. That change matters to me: making games can be a discipline people study and a career people pursue. But as games became larger entertainment products, expectations and production demands grew too.

Some projects require substantial money, time, coordination, and labor. It is understandable that a company investing heavily in a game also thinks about its audience and commercial prospects. My worry is that the safer-looking bet can become the one that promises more scale and familiar spectacle, even when the more interesting bet might be a new way to play.

I do not think the answer is to reject big games. It is to make room for different ways of making them.

## The future of video games can have more than one model

I see three broad paths—not as a prediction of what will happen, but as a useful way to think about what we might want to preserve.

**Corporate AAA development** can bring investment, specialist teams, and the resources to attempt large, technically demanding projects. It can also face incentives to reach a wide audience and manage considerable commercial risk. Those pressures do not automatically make a game less creative. But they can influence which kinds of projects are considered worth pursuing—and which older games receive continued commercial attention.

**AI-enabled independent teams** could use emerging tools to explore ideas with smaller teams or focus on niches that might not suit a major publisher. That possibility deserves curiosity, not a promise that AI will make ambitious games nearly free to create. Tools may assist with particular tasks; they do not, by themselves, supply a coherent design, capable developers, a finished production, or an audience.

There are concrete but limited examples. Ubisoft’s 2023 introduction of [Ghostwriter](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/7Cm07zbBGy4Xml6WgYi25d/the-convergence-of-ai-and-creativity-introducing-ghostwriter) described a tool that drafts NPC barks for writers to select and edit. Ubisoft presented it as a workflow aid; that description is not independent proof of cost savings. And Microsoft’s [WHAM work](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08600-3), published in *Nature* in 2025, is research into a gameplay world-and-action model—not evidence that a production-ready system can deliver an AAA game or cut a commercial game’s budget.

Developers’ views are mixed, too. In GDC’s [2025 State of the Game Industry survey](https://gdconf.com/article/gdc-2025-state-of-the-game-industry-devs-weigh-in-on-layoffs-ai-and-more/), 52% of respondents said their companies had implemented generative AI, 36% said they had used it personally, and 30% believed it was having a negative impact on the industry. Those are survey responses about adoption and opinion—not measured productivity gains or proof of job replacement. To me, the sensible position is neither “AI will fix everything” nor “AI has nothing useful to offer.” It is that people will use it in some parts of development, while its value and costs remain questions to evaluate rather than assumptions to sell.

**Community-led work** offers another model: fans and volunteers can build tools, document games, create mods, and keep technical knowledge alive. These projects do not replace the original developers, nor do they mean communities own the games they work with. They show how much care and expertise can exist outside a commercial release cycle.

The [OpenGOAL Jak and Daxter project](https://github.com/open-goal/jak-project), for example, documents a decompilation and recompilation effort to make the trilogy run natively on PC. Its project status distinguishes a polished *Jak 1*, a beta *Jak II*, and substantial work remaining on *Jak 3*. The project excludes the original copyrighted assets and requires users to supply their own legitimately acquired PS2 copy. [Ship of Harkinian](https://github.com/HarbourMasters/Shipwright) similarly excludes copyrighted game assets and requires a supported copy.

Modding can also change how players experience a game. *Elden Ring* already has co-op; the [Seamless Co-op mod](https://www.pcgamer.com/elden-rings-co-op-mod-made-the-adventure-new-again/) offers a different way to stay together through the adventure, rather than relying on repeated session restarts. That is a community-made alternative, not proof that the original game had no co-op—and, as with mods generally, compatibility and bugs can be part of the deal.

## Access is part of the future, too

Keeping games available is difficult. In a study by the Video Game History Foundation and the Software Preservation Network, 87% of a random sample of 1,500 games released in the United States before 2010 were out of print. The study’s data was current through April 15, 2023. “Out of print” describes commercial availability; it does **not** prove that a game has no surviving copies or cannot be accessed in any way. But it does show how many older games are no longer commercially available through the channels measured. ([Study and methodology](https://gamehistory.org/87percent/); [dataset](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.8161056))

Access also involves rights. In 2024, the U.S. Copyright Office did not grant a requested exemption that would have let libraries and archives provide remote access to out-of-print games under the relevant U.S. rules. That is a U.S.-specific decision, not a description of the law in every country or a verdict on every mod or decompilation project. It is a reminder that preservation depends not only on technical skill, but also on legal permissions and practical access to the material needed to maintain a game.

That is why I hope future games—and the institutions and communities around them—make room for thoughtful preservation. Not every game will stay commercially available forever. Not every project will have a straightforward route to preservation. But documentation, tools, lawful access, and continued technical work can help people study games and keep more of the medium within reach.

I do not need to own every game to want the games I love to remain accessible in some responsible way. I want future players to be able to discover them, learn from them, and decide what deserves to be remembered. Access is not the same as demanding ownership, and preservation is not a license to ignore creators’ rights.

The future I hope for is not one in which AAA studios disappear, AI-enabled independent teams replace established developers, or communities take over commercial games. It is one in which these approaches can coexist: major productions can take ambitious swings, smaller teams can pursue ideas with the tools available to them, and communities can help document and maintain the history around those games.

If we can keep more of that history accessible, we give the medium more chances to learn from itself—and more ways to survive a difficult stretch without forgetting what made games worth playing in the first place.
