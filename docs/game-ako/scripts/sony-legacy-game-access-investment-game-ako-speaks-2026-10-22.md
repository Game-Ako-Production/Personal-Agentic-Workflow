# Sony Should Bring Your Old PlayStation Games Forward | GameAko Speaks!

**Format:** Recorded YouTube commentary  
**Estimated runtime:** 10–12 minutes

## Spoken script

If you bought a game digitally ten or fifteen years ago, should you still be able to play it on the PlayStation you own today?

That sounds like a simple question. But the answer can depend on the publisher, the game’s license, the hardware it was made for, whether its servers are still running, and what Sony’s current systems support.

I understand why that uncertainty makes people distrust digital purchases. But I don’t think it’s an unsolvable problem. I think Sony should treat access to eligible legacy games as an investment in its players—and in the future of the PlayStation platform.

Let me be clear about what I’m arguing. I’m not saying Sony should be blamed every time a publisher delists a game or shuts down its servers. Publishers control many of those decisions. And I’m not saying every old game can be carried forward with one simple software update.

I am saying Sony has an important role in what happens to purchases made through PlayStation. It can make a policy, build a process, and invest in ways to keep eligible games playable as the hardware changes.

Because for a player, the key question is not just, “Is the game still in the Store?” It’s, “Can I still access something I paid for—and what options do I have when my old console is no longer practical?”

First, we should separate a few things that often get mixed together.

A game being delisted usually means new customers can no longer buy it from that store. That does not automatically mean previous buyers lose their purchase or can no longer download the game. Sony’s support information tells players to find purchases in their library and download them from there.

But that still isn’t a promise that every game will remain playable forever. A license can expire. A service can close. A game can require servers to run. And a console might not support the software in the first place.

Those are different problems, and they can have different people responsible.

Take *The Crew*. Ubisoft delisted the game and shut down its servers in 2024. Because it was an always-online game, the shutdown made the game inaccessible. That’s a serious loss for players, but it’s a publisher-led server shutdown—not proof that Sony removed PlayStation customers’ purchase entitlements. We should be precise about that distinction.

Then there’s *Metal Gear Solid 2* and *Metal Gear Solid 3*. Konami temporarily suspended sales of certain versions in 2021 while it renewed licenses for historical footage used in the games. That’s an example of how licensing can affect availability. It is not evidence that Konami patched old copies to remove the footage.

So the point isn’t that every delisting is the same, or that every lost game is Sony’s fault. The point is that digital access depends on a chain of decisions and technology. Sony owns one important part of that chain: the PlayStation platform and the experience of buying and accessing games through it.

This is also why I don’t think movie and TV examples should define the whole discussion about game ownership.

Some of the examples people raise about purchased content disappearing from Sony services involve movies and television. That can damage trust too. But games are different in ways that matter. A movie may be available through another streaming service, on disc, or through a different edition. Those options aren’t guaranteed, but they may exist.

Games can be exclusive to one platform. They can depend on unusual hardware. Their online features may be tied to specific servers. And sometimes the old version is the only version people can legally buy or play. If it becomes inaccessible, there might not be a substitute at all.

That is especially important as consoles move forward. Sony has announced staged closures for the PS3 and Vita Stores, beginning in some regions in 2026 and planned for other regions in 2027. The company says previously purchased content will remain downloadable for the foreseeable future. That commitment is helpful. But downloading a game onto old hardware is not the same as carrying it forward to current hardware.

The PS5 does not support PS3 discs. And a digital PS3 or Vita purchase does not automatically become a PS5 game. So players may still have the purchase in their old library and still have no supported way to play it on their newer console.

To me, that is the missing piece in Sony’s digital strategy: not just keeping access alive on the old machine, but creating a path for eligible purchases to keep moving forward.

And there are several ways Sony could do that.

One path is local emulation: letting newer hardware reproduce the older console environment so the game runs on the current device. That could be a strong option for players who want to download and play locally. But the work varies by system and by game. PS3 emulation is particularly challenging because its architecture is unusual. We shouldn’t pretend every PS3 game can be made to run flawlessly on a PS5 with no effort.

A second path is a native port or recompilation. For some games, Sony or the publisher could adapt the game to newer hardware. That might be the most reliable option for important titles, but it can require real engineering work, testing, and permission from whoever controls the rights.

A third path is cloud streaming. Sony already streams selected classic games, including selected PS3 titles, through PlayStation Plus Premium in supported regions. That shows the basic delivery model is already part of PlayStation’s business. But streaming a game from a catalog is not the same as letting someone stream every game they bought years ago. The selection is limited, availability changes, and it varies by region.

So the proposal isn’t “Sony should make every old game run everywhere tomorrow.” It’s that Sony should create a clear, durable process. Tell players which purchases qualify. Explain which games can be played locally, which can be streamed, which need a new port, and which can’t be carried forward because of rights or technical barriers.

And when a game can’t move forward, be transparent about why.

AI may help reduce parts of the engineering workload. It could assist with code analysis, compatibility work, or porting tools. But AI cannot renew a publisher’s license, test every game thoroughly, or make server infrastructure free. It could make the work more efficient, but it doesn’t remove the investment.

That brings us to the business question: can Sony afford to make this a priority?

Sony’s reported FY2025 Game and Network Services segment had about 4.69 trillion yen in sales and 463.3 billion yen in operating income, which Sony described as a record for the segment. In the first quarter of FY2026, PlayStation reported 125 million monthly active users. Sony has also said it wants to increase engagement with PlayStation Plus and maximize average revenue per user on the PlayStation Store.

Those numbers show scale. They don’t tell us how much a legacy access program would cost, or prove that the investment would pay for itself. Sony has other priorities too. Preserving games takes money, staff, rights agreements, testing, and possibly ongoing server support.

But I think it makes sense to evaluate this as a platform investment, not only as a cost.

We live in an attention economy. But attention by itself is not revenue. It becomes valuable when people stay engaged: when they return to the platform, subscribe, buy games, purchase add-ons, or recommend PlayStation to other players.

A library that respects a player’s past purchases could give someone a reason to return. It could help them discover older games they already own, explore a new subscription tier, or buy new games on the same platform. That’s a possible business benefit—not a guaranteed result—but Sony already says engagement and PlayStation Store spending matter to its strategy.

There’s a trust condition, though. Sony cannot build goodwill by asking people to pay again for the same game they already bought. And if the only way to access an old purchase on modern hardware is through a subscription, some players may feel that their ownership has been turned into another recurring fee.

The foundation has to be respect for the original purchase. Then Sony can offer optional ways to play—through emulation, ports, or cloud streaming—where the rights and technology allow.

The more honest answer is that digital purchases come with uncertainty. But the answer shouldn’t stop there. Sony can reduce that uncertainty with a policy, a process, and a real investment in compatibility and preservation.

Publishers will still control their content and their servers. Some games will remain difficult or impossible to bring forward. But Sony can make it easier for players to understand what they bought, what they can still access, and what the company is doing to preserve eligible games as generations change.

For me, that would be a meaningful way to rebuild trust: don’t just sell players a digital library. Show them that PlayStation has a plan for that library to last.

What do you think? Should Sony let eligible digital purchases carry forward to newer PlayStation hardware, even if it has to use different methods for different games? Tell me in the comments. This is Game Ako, and I’ll see you in the next one.
