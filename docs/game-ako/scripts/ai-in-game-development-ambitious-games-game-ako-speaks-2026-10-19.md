[Opening]

What if the next ambitious game doesn’t come from a giant studio—not because big studios can’t make great games, but because a small team, a creator, or a community finally has enough tools to try?

That possibility is exciting. But there’s a catch: making a game is only part of the story. Who owns it? Who maintains it? And what happens when the original creator, company, or platform moves on?

Today, I want to talk about how AI and community development might change where ambitious games get made—and why preservation should be part of the plan from the beginning.

First, let’s be clear about what I’m not saying. AI has not made a small team equal to a AAA studio. It hasn’t erased the cost of making a large game, and it doesn’t automatically give a community the right or ability to preserve one. My view is narrower, and more hopeful: tools can lower particular barriers, while creative ambition can exist at many different scales.

[Transition]

Games have always depended on two things: someone with a creative ambition, and someone willing to back it.

That doesn’t mean older games were made outside commercial logic. Publishers and businesses still made bets. But some of those bets began with a creator pushing for a particular vision.

Take *Final Fantasy*. In a 2007 *Famitsu* interview, Hironobu Sakaguchi reportedly said that if the game failed, he would leave the industry and return to university. The project was backed after *Dragon Quest* had helped validate the RPG market. That’s a creator-led gamble—but one that also had a commercial context.

Or consider Hideo Kojima discussing *Metal Gear Solid*. In an interview translated by Shmuplations, he described the PlayStation as a chance to realize the original vision and talked about wanting a cinematic experience while keeping the game fun to play. I’m paraphrasing his ideas here, not repeating them word for word. The point is that a creator saw a new platform as an opportunity to attempt something ambitious—and a publisher backed the project.

These examples aren’t proof that the past was automatically more creative. They’re a reminder that ambitious games have often needed both a strong vision and the support to make that vision possible.

[Transition]

Now jump to the present, where the scale of some productions can be enormous.

The UK Competition and Markets Authority’s 2023 report cited publishers describing budgets of more than 200 million dollars for some AAA games. That’s some games—not every AAA game, and certainly not a universal price tag. But it gives us a sense of how expensive the upper end can get.

And when a project demands a huge team, a long schedule, specialized expertise, and substantial investment, the risks change. A company may become more cautious about which ideas it funds. That doesn’t mean large studios can’t take creative risks. They can—and they still make games with distinctive ideas. It just means that the cost of a particular ambition may affect whether someone is willing to back it.

For Filipino gamers, there are different routes into making games, too. CHED’s 2014 standards for a Bachelor of Science in Entertainment and Multimedia Computing include game development and digital animation. That’s one formal educational route among many—not evidence that it’s the only route, or the most common one. People can also learn through independent projects, other courses, communities, and work experience.

The important point is that the skills and tools for making games don’t belong to one kind of studio alone.

So what happens when tools help people do more with less time or fewer specialized resources?

[AI section]

AI is part of that conversation, but we need to be precise about what the evidence does—and doesn’t—show.

The GDC 2026 State of the Game Industry survey reports on AI use and career experiences in the industry. It describes people using AI for tasks such as research, code assistance, and prototyping. That tells us AI is entering some workflows. It does not establish that AI has reduced AAA budgets, or that it has made a small team equivalent to a large one.

That distinction matters. A tool might help someone explore an idea, get past a technical roadblock, or speed up a limited task. But a game is not just a pile of tasks. It needs design decisions, a coherent direction, iteration, testing, production, and people who take responsibility for the result. AI assistance doesn’t automatically provide that judgment—or make the work good.

My view is that AI could stretch a small team’s capacity in specific areas. That’s a possibility, not a proven guarantee that every small team will suddenly make its dream game. It also doesn’t answer who owns the work, what material went into a model, or whether the result is something the community trusts.

Which brings me to a framework that helps keep these questions separate. Think of game development across three overlapping dimensions—not three steps in a timeline, and not three exclusive categories.

First: who funds the project?

Second: how is it made? That includes the tools, the team, and the production workflow.

Third: who controls and maintains it after release?

AI can change the second dimension: how work gets done. But it doesn’t automatically change who funds the game, who owns it, or who has the right to maintain it. A company can use AI and still control the project. A community can build a game without using AI. And a small creator might use AI tools while still needing a publisher, a platform, or other partners.

These dimensions overlap. They don’t form a simple path from solo creator to studio to community. And none of them guarantees creative success.

My own bet is that creative games can flourish at different scales. Large studios can still make ambitious work. Small teams may use new tools to stretch their capacity. And communities can create projects that continue beyond one company or one creator. That’s a hopeful hypothesis—not a claim that small projects are automatically better, or that corporate games and sequels are inherently uncreative.

[Transition to OpenTTD]

For a useful preservation example, look at *OpenTTD*.

According to OpenTTD’s own history, it began in 2002 as an individual project by Ludvig Strigeus. It became open source in 2004 under the GPL version 2 license. So it wasn’t a community project from day one. The community became part of the story over time.

One important milestone came in 2010, when community-created OpenGFX, OpenSFX, and OpenMSX made it possible to release version 1.0.0 as a fully standalone game, without requiring the original game data. The project also grew with features such as multiplayer, modding, and custom AIs.

That’s preservation by design in a meaningful sense: the project developed a technical path and replacement assets that let people keep playing and contributing without relying on the original game data. But that doesn’t mean the original game was legally abandoned. *OpenTTD*’s story is not a magic permission slip for copying any game whose rights are complicated.

And open source doesn’t solve every access problem. Atari acquired *Transport Tycoon* in 2024. Then, in 2026, OpenTTD announced changes to its Steam availability, followed by an update. The game remains free from the OpenTTD website. The details matter, and the official project announcements are the right place to check for current access information.

The broader lesson is that code, art, sound, trademarks, distribution, and rights can all have different stories. Opening source code can make a project more maintainable, but it doesn’t make every other barrier disappear.

[Community AI debate]

That becomes especially important as AI tools enter community projects.

AI didn’t invent decompilation or reverse engineering. Human-led tools and projects existed long before the current wave of AI agents. Recent AI-assisted activity may have accelerated some work, but it would be wrong to credit AI with creating the broader scene—or to dismiss years of human contribution as if none of it mattered.

The decomp.dev maintainer described a difficult environment involving rushed ports, hostile forks, and confusion between serious decompilation work and quick PC ports. That is the maintainer’s account, not an independently measured summary of every project in the scene. But it points to an important distinction: careful reverse engineering is not the same thing as generating a rough port and presenting it as equivalent work.

When contributions arrive faster than maintainers can review them, more activity doesn’t necessarily mean more progress. Projects still need people who understand the code, review changes, manage rights and provenance concerns, and maintain what gets accepted. Credit matters, too—especially when new tools build on work that came before.

And communities don’t all have the same position on AI.

Godot’s contribution policy sets strict limits: autonomous agents and vibe coding are prohibited, substantial code should be human-authored, and some limited, menial AI assistance is allowed. The policy cites concerns that include review burden and whether contributors understand what they submit.

OpenTTD’s contribution guidelines reject LLM-generated issues and pull requests. Other communities take different approaches. Some emphasize provenance and copyright; some may allow disclosed AI assistance when a human has reviewed and understands the work.

We shouldn’t flatten all of those concerns into “people just hate AI.” Review capacity, maintainability, credit, authorship, and contributor understanding are real questions for a project that has to keep working after the initial excitement fades.

[Closing]

So where does that leave us?

I think AI and accessible tools may help more people attempt ambitious work. But they don’t eliminate the need for funding, craft, testing, trust, or long-term care. And a community-built project is not automatically a healthy project. It needs clear rights, shared knowledge, contribution rules, and maintainers who can carry it forward.

*OpenTTD* shows what can happen when a project develops ways for people to play, contribute, and preserve it beyond its original creator. The current debates around AI-assisted contributions show why that kind of stewardship takes more than producing code quickly.

My bet is that we’ll see creative games emerge at different scales: from big studios, small teams, and communities. Not because any one approach guarantees a better game, but because different structures make different kinds of work possible. The hopeful part isn’t that one model replaces the others. It’s that more people might get a chance to try—and that we can build preservation and responsibility into the work, rather than treating them as problems for later.

What do you think? Can AI help small teams make more ambitious games, or do you think the bigger challenge is ownership and long-term maintenance? And when a community project uses AI, what rules should it have around disclosure, review, and credit?
