## Model A — Corporate AAA

Games driven by commercial investment
Large studios continue producing technically demanding, expensive games. Their resources allow for sophisticated graphics, extensive content, large development teams, and global marketing.
However, increasing production costs can encourage risk aversion. Established franchises, remakes, remasters, and familiar gameplay formulas can become more attractive than experimental projects.
Preservation may also become commercially selective: publishers are more likely to revisit games with recognizable brands and profitable audiences than games with historical or artistic significance but limited commercial potential.

## Model B — AI-enabled independent studios

Games driven by creative ambition
Small teams use AI-assisted development, reusable tools, and automated testing to create ambitious experiences without needing the resources of a traditional AAA studio.
These developers can serve niche audiences, experiment with unusual mechanics, and create games that do not need to appeal to millions of people to become sustainable.
They can also make preservation a production priority by documenting dependencies, supporting offline play, maintaining build instructions, and minimizing unnecessary reliance on proprietary services.

## Model C — Community-owned development

Games driven by shared ownership and participation
Communities contribute to open-source engines, preservation tools, game modifications, documentation, original games, and long-term maintenance.
Projects can continue after their original creators leave, provided the community has the necessary rights, source materials, knowledge, and resources.
Preservation becomes a collective responsibility rather than something dependent on a corporation's commercial priorities.

These models can coexist. The important change is that Models B and C could expand the range of games that can be created and maintained outside traditional corporate structures.
