## 1. NetEase, Nagoshi Studio, and selective investment — 10–12 minutes

**Verified facts:** Nagoshi Studio was dissolved following an October 31 shareholders’ resolution. NetEase’s latest available results show Games and related value-added services revenue grew **9.7% year over year to RMB 25.0 billion** in Q2 2026. First-half revenue grew **8.3% to RMB 50.7 billion**. NetEase credited growth to self-developed games including *Where Winds Meet* and *Fantasy Westward Journey*.

**Host talk track:** “Here’s the tension: NetEase’s game revenue is growing, but it stopped funding one overseas studio, which has now dissolved. That doesn’t mean its whole games business is shrinking. It does raise questions about which projects and studios the company considers worth continuing to fund.”

**Keep these claims qualified:** Bloomberg reported in March, citing anonymous sources, that NetEase would stop funding the studio and that *Gang of Dragon* needed about ¥7 billion more to reach completion. The estimate is not a company-confirmed figure. The game’s future is unresolved in the sources checked, so don’t call it officially cancelled. The claims that labor-cost advantages, AI production, or a specific strategy shift caused the closure are not established by the evidence reviewed.

**Co-host prompts:**

- “What would you want to know before calling this a retreat from global development?”
- “Does a studio closure tell us more about one game’s risk, or about a publisher’s wider strategy?”
- “If publishers put more money into established live games, what might that mean for new original projects?”

**Transition:** “A studio can close while its parent company’s game revenue grows. Let’s look at the other side of the risk equation: what happens when a game launches strongly?”

## 3. PlayStation and Nintendo: hardware, software, and engagement — 10–12 minutes

**Verified facts:** In Sony’s July–September quarter, its Game & Network Services division reported **¥1.2085 trillion in sales** and **¥220.1 billion in operating income**. Sony shipped **2.6 million PS5s**, down from 4 million in the same quarter a year earlier. PlayStation had **127 million monthly active users** in September, while PS4 and PS5 full-game sales reached **88.2 million units** for the quarter.

Nintendo’s latest report covers **April–September**, a longer period than Sony’s quarter. Nintendo reported **6.49 million Switch 2 hardware units**, down 37.4% year over year, while Switch 2 software sales grew 18.8% to **24.49 million units**. Nintendo’s net sales fell 12.1%, but operating profit increased 49.1%; the company said that increase partly reflected a U.S. tariff refund.

**Host talk track:** “Hardware is only one part of platform health. Sony’s latest figures show lower PS5 shipments than a year ago, alongside growth in PlayStation activity and full-game sales. Nintendo’s six-month results show the same broad tension between hardware and software. The reporting periods differ, so these numbers aren’t a direct head-to-head comparison.”

## 4. AI market sell-off: bubble burst, or correction? — 7–9 minutes

**Scope:** Keep this short and tie it to gaming’s hardware and infrastructure ecosystem. The AI market is broader than Game Ako’s usual coverage.

**Verified facts:** On November 5, the Nasdaq closed **11.9% below its October record**, and the S&P 500 fell **4.4% that day**. Coverage points to several concerns—including AI infrastructure spending, expected returns, financing, and weaker semiconductor results—not simply major technology companies missing earnings expectations. For example, Amazon reported AWS revenue growth of 39.3%, while its free cash flow fell; Amazon attributed this mainly to higher capital expenditure.

IDC identifies physical constraints on AI infrastructure, including data-center capacity, power, cooling, connectivity, and equipment lead times. That supports a discussion of how physical infrastructure takes time to build. It does **not** establish that there is enough capacity for current AI demand overall.

**Host talk track:** “A stock sell-off tells us that investors are reassessing risk and expected returns. By itself, it doesn’t prove that an AI bubble has burst—or that demand for AI is gone. Software can scale quickly, but data centers need sites, equipment, and power.”

**Suggested angle for Arohbi to accept, change, or reject:** “Instead of asking whether this is definitely a bubble, we could ask which parts of the build-out have real demand—and whether they can earn enough to justify the spending.”

**Co-host prompts:**

- “What would convince you the build-out is running ahead of its likely returns?”
- “How could chip or infrastructure constraints affect gaming hardware or game development?”
- “Can we tell a normal correction from a bubble while it’s still happening?”

**Avoid presenting as fact:** “The bubble has burst,” “it’s only a correction,” “there’s enough data-center capacity,” or the dot-com and railway comparisons. Those are interpretations or analogies.

**Sources:** [AP on the November 5 sell-off](https://apnews.com/article/7ee9c212805aa5e70cf34d25aab7a36e) · [IDC on AI capacity constraints](https://www.idc.com/resource-center/blog/ai-is-short-of-capacity-not-demand/) · [Barclays on AI spending and funding concerns](https://privatebank.barclays.com/insights/ai-sell-off-11-2026/) · [Amazon’s Q3 results](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-earnings-q3-2026-report)
